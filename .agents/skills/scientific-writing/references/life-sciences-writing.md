# Life-Sciences Writing

Use this reference only for life-sciences or scientific artifacts. It is a presentation layer: preserve the supplied scientific meaning and evidence rather than fact-checking, expanding claims, or performing citation validation.

## Voice and audience

Write by default for a graduate researcher, PI, or similarly expert reader in biology, biomedicine, or a neighboring life-sciences field. Combine broad conceptual framing with a clear mechanistic narrative: establish the scientific question, make the conceptual advance legible, and show the experimental or evidentiary logic without hype or imitation of any journal’s wording.

Retain established specialist terms. Define only terms that are unusually specialized, ambiguous, newly introduced, or likely unfamiliar to an interdisciplinary reader.

## Structure and sections

Choose the structure that serves the requested artifact. A literature synthesis, conclusions document, internal report, or evidence summary is not a manuscript by default.

Apply section-specific conventions only when that section exists:

- In abstracts or summaries, foreground the central question, principal finding, and significance within the available evidence.
- In results or completed-experiment descriptions, normally use past tense and lead with the finding before necessary detail.
- In discussion or interpretation, distinguish observations from interpretation through precise wording and logical ordering; do not independently soften, challenge, or add caveats to supplied claims.
- Make figure legends understandable with the figure when figures are present, while preserving required format and identifiers.

Where no user, journal, template, or project policy specifies otherwise, use Chicago author-date citations and an alphabetized reference list for academic citations. Citation provenance is outside this reference.
